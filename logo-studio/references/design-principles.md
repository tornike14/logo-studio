# Design principles

How to think, so the logo comes from the brand and not from a template.

## Start from what the person does

Do not begin with the category or a style. Ask what actually happens when someone uses this: what they do with their hands, what changes for them, what they come back for. A good mark is often a picture of that moment reduced to almost nothing.

A coffee subscription and a meditation app can both be drawn as a circle. What separates them is the idea behind the circle. "The mark is the dark ring a cup leaves on a table" is an idea. "A warm, friendly circle" is a mood, and a mood can belong to anyone.

## One idea

A mark holds one idea. If you need two sentences to explain it, remove something. Try taking away each part: if the idea survives, that part was decoration. The test for finished work is that it feels inevitable, as if no other answer was possible.

Simplicity is not removing things until it looks clean. It is finding the structure that already explains itself. Care matters more than novelty: novelty ages, and care does not. The smallest size and the least visible detail deserve the same attention as the hero image, because people feel the difference even when they cannot name it.

## Geometry should settle itself

Build from a rule: one axis, one ratio, one repeated step. If a measurement cannot be explained, it is probably wrong. Then adjust by eye only where the eye disagrees with the maths: a circle needs to sit a little low to look centred, and points overshoot. Record these corrections, since they go on the page.

## Colour

One idea can usually be carried by one colour family and one accent. Use colours the brand already owns before inventing new ones. A logo must work in a single colour first, because that proves the shape carries the idea, and colour is then a bonus.

## Keep what is worth keeping

If there is a current logo, find what is good in it. An old mark usually holds one true thought in a weak form. Carrying that forward gives the new mark a history and the page its best line.

## Avoid the crowded places

Gate 3 shows where a category has gathered, and those places cost distinctiveness however good the drawing is. These cover any category: a letter in a shape, a generic heart, leaf, spark or sun, a blurred gradient blob, a globe, a swoosh, a hexagon for technology. They are not forbidden, but choosing one needs a strong reason from the brief.

## Reach for the unusual view

Good ideas often come from changing the point of view: look into something instead of at it, draw the space around an object, draw the moment before or after, draw the structure instead of the surface. If every competitor shows a thing from the front, there may be room in showing it from above.

## Letters can be the logo

A symbol is not always the answer. When a name is short, or written in a script with real character, the strongest logo is often the word itself with the idea drawn into one letter: a stroke that becomes the object, a rule that finishes the word. Lettering also solves a problem that symbols cannot, because people read the name every time they see it.

Treat scripts other than Latin with respect. Lead with the language the customer reads, and draw the letters from a good font's real structure. Have a native reader check them before anything is printed.

## Name the mark

Give the idea a short name. It keeps the work honest, and it helps the page and the team talk about it.
